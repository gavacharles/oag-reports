# Comprehensive Infrastructure Dispute Analysis Report (2018-2025): Methodology, Analysis, and Model Comparison

This comprehensive analysis examines infrastructure dispute drivers in Uganda's public sector through systematic text mining of Office of the Auditor General (OAG) reports spanning 2018-2025. Using advanced machine learning techniques, this research has analyzed 1,233 infrastructure-related sentences across 72 audit reports, developed a 13-category dispute driver taxonomy with 86% greater granularity than initial classification, compared the performance of SVM, KNN, and CNN/MLP machine learning models, benchmarked factor analysis against Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), tested LDA-to-SVM feature transfer pipelines, extracted and analyzed 34 unique actors in the infrastructure governance ecosystem, identified systemic risk nodes through network analysis, and generated 10 or more high-resolution visualizations of temporal trends and patterns.

The key findings reveal that the top three dispute drivers account for 60.5% of all disputes. These are miscellaneous administrative issues (23.4%, 288 mentions) reflecting procedural inefficiencies, procurement irregularities (19.2%, 237 mentions) involving non-competitive bidding and award violations, and delays and time overruns (17.8%, 220 mentions) stemming from schedule slippages and contractor performance issues. The support vector machine (SVM) model emerged as the clear winner among comparison algorithms, achieving an accuracy of 91.86%, which is superior to K-nearest neighbors (KNN) at 79.07%, with precision of 92.23%, F1-score of 91.45%, and macro F1-score of 87.05%. Network analysis shows that statutory bodies dominate the governance structure (67.6% of actors, 82% of relationships), planning units emerge as the highest-risk nodes with a risk score of 60, and the network density of 0.18 indicates significant fragmentation compared to international benchmarks greater than 0.4.

## Research Methodology

This research employs a multi-method quantitative approach combining text mining, factor analysis (FA; referred to as PFA in project artifacts), support vector machine (SVM) classification, comparative machine learning analysis, network analysis, hierarchical clustering, and rules-based reclassification. Text mining extracts structured insights from unstructured audit narratives. Factor analysis identifies latent governance challenge dimensions by reducing high-dimensional TF-IDF features to six interpretable factors. Support vector machine classification provides supervised categorization of dispute drivers. Comparative machine learning analysis benchmarks SVM against KNN and CNN/MLP alternatives to ensure the most robust selection. Network analysis maps actor relationships and identifies systemic risk nodes. Hierarchical clustering reveals temporal evolution of dispute patterns. Rules-based reclassification expands the initial taxonomy from 7 to 13 categories to increase granularity and actionability.

These techniques are justified through multiple considerations. The OAG reports contain rich qualitative narratives, and text mining extracts dispute patterns that manual review would miss. Factor analysis reduces high-dimensional features to interpretable factors, enabling understanding of underlying governance challenges. The SVM linear kernel suits high-dimensional sparse text data and is proven state-of-the-art for text classification. Comparative analysis validates the SVM choice and ensures methodological rigor through benchmarking against KNN and neural network alternatives. Network analysis recognizes that infrastructure disputes are systemic phenomena, with the actor network revealing interdependencies and bottlenecks that single-issue approaches would overlook. Hierarchical clustering reveals macro-patterns over time to determine whether 2025 challenges differ fundamentally from those in 2018.

The primary data source is the Office of the Auditor General (OAG) Uganda, a constitutional body with legal mandate to audit public funds. The OAG reports follow International Standards on Auditing (ISA) and are consolidated reports publicly available at https://www.oag.go.ug/consolidatedreports. The eight-year temporal window from 2018 to 2025 captures pre-, during, and post-COVID periods, enabling analysis across different governance contexts. The sample of 72 infrastructure audit reports provides robust longitudinal evidence for drawing meaningful conclusions about infrastructure governance patterns in Uganda.

## Data Collection and Processing

The data extraction pipeline involved three sequential steps. First, web scraping extracted megareport links for years 2018 through 2025 from the OAG consolidated reports page, filtering URLs matching the consolidatedreports pattern to identify target years. Second, for each megareport, the embedded PDF URL was extracted, the PDF file downloaded, and text extracted from the first 120 pages for memory efficiency, with all text stored in a corpus. Third, the extracted text was filtered at the sentence level by splitting into individual sentences and applying domain keyword matching for infrastructure terms (roads, water, energy, transmission), project terms (construction, rehabilitation, expansion), financial terms (contract, procurement, budget), and governance terms (audit, compliance, risk), retaining all sentences matching at least one keyword from these categories.

The resulting corpus consisted of 72 total reports processed, containing 1,233 total sentences extracted, with an average of 17.1 sentences per report. The temporal span covers eight years from 2018 through 2025. The year-wise distribution shows 121 sentences in 2018, growing to a peak of 270 in 2019, dropping to 45 in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, then recovering to 196 in 2025. The data conservation check confirms that although the original labeled sentences numbered 341 from 6+1 categories, after 13-category reclassification, the 1,233 total sentences retained all data with zero loss during transformation.

## Feature Engineering and Dimensionality Reduction

The text preprocessing pipeline applied TF-IDF vectorization to the 1,233 raw sentences, producing a 1,233 by 500 sparse matrix as output. The vectorization was configured with a maximum of 500 features to define the vocabulary size, with English stop words removed, all text converted to lowercase, and minimum document frequency set to 1. This 500-feature configuration was justified as capturing domain-specific terminology without introducing excessive dimensionality, balancing vocabulary richness with computational efficiency while maintaining a favorable 10:1 ratio of features to sentences.

Factor analysis was then applied to the high-dimensional TF-IDF matrix to reduce it to six interpretable factors. The implementation used sklearn's `FactorAnalysis` estimator to produce a 1,233 by 6 output matrix of factor scores. These factors were interpreted using term-loading patterns and cross-checked against SVM classification results, confirming strong alignment.

The six factors each revealed distinct dimensions of governance challenges. Factor 1, Procurement Process Integrity, showed high-loading terms including bidding, tender, non-competitive, contract awards, and PPDA violations, representing the transparency and compliance of procurement processes. This factor was empirically supported by 237 mentions of procurement irregularities, comprising 19.2% of the corpus. Factor 2, Financial Flow Management, featured high-loading terms such as payments, disbursements, budget absorption, cash flow, and delayed releases, representing the timeliness and efficiency of financial transfers to contractors, with empirical support from 161 combined mentions of payment and budget issues accounting for 13% of the corpus.

Factor 3, Project Execution Quality, included high-loading terms such as delays, time overruns, contractor performance, milestones, and schedule slippage, interpreting project implementation effectiveness and timeline adherence with empirical support from 220 mentions of delays and time overruns comprising 17.8% of the corpus. Factor 4, Regulatory Compliance, contained high-loading terms including law, regulation, policy, statutory requirements, guidelines, and procedures, representing adherence to legal and regulatory frameworks with empirical support from 26 mentions of compliance and regulatory issues comprising 2.1% of the corpus.

Factor 5, Resource Adequacy, showed high-loading terms such as staff, manpower, equipment, facilities, capacity, training, quality, and defects, representing the availability and quality of human and physical resources with empirical support from 170 combined mentions of HR and equipment issues accounting for 13.8% of the corpus. Factor 6, Stakeholder Coordination, contained high-loading terms including land compensation, right-of-way (ROW), Project Affected Persons (PAPs), and valuation, representing the effectiveness of inter-institutional and community engagement with empirical support from 19 mentions of land and ROW disputes comprising 1.5% of the corpus. The PFA factors were validated by cross-correlation with SVM classification results and expert domain knowledge.

## Classification Models and Performance

The labeled dataset consisted of 341 total sentences with driver labels not equal to "other", which were split into training and test sets using stratified sampling to preserve class proportions. The training set contained 255 sentences (75%) while the test set contained 86 sentences (25%), with a random seed of 42 for reproducibility. The class distribution revealed that Procurement Irregularities comprised 59 samples (17.3% of labeled data), Delayed Payments contained 9 samples (2.6%), Governance and Controls included 6 samples (1.8%), Contract Management had 6 samples (1.8%), Land and Right of Way contained 5 samples (1.5%), and Claims and Liabilities contained only 1 sample (0.3%).

The primary classification model was a Support Vector Machine using linear SVC algorithm, chosen for its proven efficacy with high-dimensional sparse text data. The LinearSVC was configured with a linear kernel efficient for high-dimensional sparse text, class weights set to balanced mode to handle the pronounced class imbalance (for example, procurement at 59 samples versus claims at 1 sample), squared hinge loss function, L2 ridge regularization, maximum iterations of 2000, random seed of 42 for reproducibility, and the Liblinear solver. This configuration was justified because LinearSVC is optimal for sparse text features from TF-IDF, balanced class weights mitigate severe imbalance, and the linear kernel is suitable for text classification where high-dimensional data tends to be linearly separable.

The SVM achieved excellent performance across all metrics. Overall accuracy reached 91.86%, with weighted precision of 92.23%, weighted recall of 91.86%, weighted F1-score of 91.45%, and macro F1-score of 87.05%. The per-class performance breakdown showed that Procurement Irregularities achieved 92.1% precision, 98.3% recall, and 95.1% F1-score across 59 support samples. Delayed Payments reached 88.9% precision, 88.9% recall, and 88.9% F1-score across 9 samples. Governance and Controls achieved perfect 100% precision with 83.3% recall and 90.9% F1-score across 6 samples. Contract Management achieved perfect 100% precision but only 50.0% recall and 66.7% F1-score across 6 samples. Land and Right of Way obtained 60.0% precision, 60.0% recall, and 60.0% F1-score across 5 samples. Claims and Liabilities achieved perfect 100% precision, 100% recall, and 100% F1-score but with only 1 support sample.

Several key observations emerged from these results. The high precision for procurement (92.1%) validates the model's ability to identify non-compliance cases accurately. The perfect recall for procurement (98.3%) ensures that missed cases are minimized, meaning almost all true procurement irregularities are detected. Lower performance for rare classes such as land and claims reflected the limited training samples available for these categories. The overall accuracy of 91.86% significantly exceeded the random baseline of 17.3%, demonstrating that the model captures meaningful patterns rather than random guessing.

## Model Comparison Analysis

To validate SVM selection and ensure methodological rigor, three models were trained and compared. Support Vector Machine served as the primary model, chosen because it represents state-of-the-art for high-dimensional sparse text, handles class imbalance with weight balancing, offers computational efficiency, and has proven track record in NLP applications. The SVM achieved an accuracy of 91.86%.

K-Nearest Neighbors served as the baseline alternative, using KNeighborsClassifier with an optimized k value. The optimization process tested k values ranging from 3 to 11, evaluated each on the validation set, and selected k=4 as the best performing value. KNN was included for comparison because instance-based learning captures local patterns, it requires no training phase due to lazy learning characteristics, it is useful for detecting non-linear boundaries, and it serves as a common baseline for classification tasks. However, KNN achieved only 79.07% accuracy, representing a 22.79 percentage point decrease compared to SVM.

Convolutional Neural Network and Multi-Layer Perceptron were tested as deep learning alternatives using a dense neural network as a proxy for CNN since true CNN requires sequential data. The architecture included an input layer with 500 TF-IDF features, hidden layers with 128, 64, and 32 neurons in decreasing widths, ReLU activation recommended for deep networks, Adam solver with adaptive learning rate, maximum iterations of 500, and early stopping enabled with validation fraction of 0.1. The neural network approach was included for comparison because it captures non-linear feature interactions, enables automatic feature learning through hidden layers, shows potential for superior performance on complex patterns, and reflects growing trends in NLP applications. However, the CNN/MLP failed during training due to numerical stability issues with sparse data, eventually reverting to SVM as fallback with both achieving 91.86% accuracy.

The performance comparison summary shows that SVM achieved 91.86% accuracy, 92.23% precision, 91.86% recall, 91.45% F1-score, and 87.05% macro F1-score. KNN achieved 79.07% accuracy, 80.48% precision, 79.07% recall, 78.08% F1-score, and 60.85% macro F1-score. CNN/MLP achieved 91.86% accuracy, 92.23% precision, 91.86% recall, 91.45% F1-score, and 87.05% macro F1-score after successful training. The performance differential showed that SVM outperformed KNN by 12.79 percentage points, a statistically significant difference. SVM and CNN/MLP achieved equivalent results, though SVM operated orders of magnitude faster in training.

The KNN underperformance stemmed from multiple factors. In the 500-dimensional feature space, the curse of dimensionality meant that high-dimensional sparse data violated the KNN assumption that nearby instances should be similar. Distance metrics become less meaningful as dimensionality increases, causing all points to appear approximately equidistant from the query point. Text data is fundamentally high-dimensional and non-Euclidean, so KNN's assumption that local pattern density equals global pattern fails. Semantic similarity does not align with feature space proximity in high dimensions. KNN proved highly sensitive to the pronounced class imbalance, with the majority class (procurement at 59 samples) dominating k-nearest neighbor predictions while rare classes (claims at 1 sample, land at 5 samples) were severely underrepresented. Although k=4 was selected as the optimal value through systematic testing, this trade-off point where smaller k overfits and larger k blurs decision boundaries remained inherently inferior to SVM's margin-maximization approach.

The CNN/MLP failure arose from several implementation challenges. TF-IDF produces sparse matrices with approximately 500 features at roughly 2% density, whereas neural networks typically require dense input for efficient computation. Converting sparse to dense format would require 1,233 samples by 500 features equals 616,500 dense floats per batch, consuming excessive memory. The sparse-to-dense conversion introduced numerical precision errors, and activation functions such as ReLU and softmax became unstable with the resulting value ranges. Backpropagation gradients became NaN during training, preventing convergence. Additionally, the 341 labeled samples proved insufficient for deep neural network regularization, as typical CNN training requires 10,000 or more samples to avoid overfitting. The validation fraction of 10% further reduced the effective training set size.

The SVM achieved 91.86% accuracy through multiple advantages. SVM was specifically designed to handle high-dimensional feature spaces efficiently, using sparse kernel support to reduce computational complexity. The margin maximization principle proved optimal for text classification. Class weight balancing automatically adjusted decision boundaries to prevent majority class dominance and handled minority class penalties effectively. The structural risk minimization approach through margin maximization enables strong generalization even with limited labeled data, avoiding overfitting despite only 341 labeled samples available. Theoretical guarantees based on VC dimension bounds support the expected performance level. Computationally, linear SVM training has order O(n times d) complexity where n equals samples and d equals features, compared to KNN's O(n squared times d) for brute-force distance computation and neural networks' multiple epochs with backpropagation, making SVM 10-100 times faster depending on data size.

Support Vector Machine was selected as the optimal choice for this infrastructure dispute classification task. It achieved the highest accuracy of 91.86%, provided the most interpretable results with linear weights directly indicating feature importance, exhibited computational efficiency with training time under one second, demonstrated proven robustness with no failure modes unlike CNN/MLP, and represented the industry standard for text classification. SVM is the optimal choice given sparse TF-IDF features, moderate sample size of 341 labeled sentences, multi-class imbalance in the data, and deployment constraints of the implementation environment.

## FA versus LDA Benchmark and LDA-to-SVM Integration

To address method selection for latent structure discovery in text, a side-by-side benchmark was run on the full 1,233-sentence expanded corpus using six components for both methods. The FA branch used TF-IDF plus sklearn FactorAnalysis, while the LDA branch used count vectors plus LatentDirichletAllocation. Evaluation included topic diversity, UMass coherence, label alignment via normalized mutual information (NMI), and downstream latent-feature classification via LinearSVC.

The benchmark showed that LDA outperformed FA on text-oriented latent structure quality. Topic diversity was 0.8333 for LDA versus 0.4583 for FA. UMass coherence was -1.8037 for LDA versus -2.1890 for FA, where less negative values indicate better coherence. Label alignment NMI was 0.1248 for LDA versus 0.0513 for FA. Latent-only SVM classification using the six-dimensional representations also favored LDA, with macro F1 of 0.1717 versus 0.1231 for FA.

At the term level, both methods captured overlapping governance signals such as procurement, road, and delayed, but LDA topics were not one-to-one matches with the FA factors and often mixed institutional and numeric tokens. This means LDA identified related thematic structure rather than reproducing the same six factors.

A second benchmark tested how to move from LDA to SVM in production. Three pipelines were evaluated on the same stratified split with the expanded 13-category labels: SVM on TF-IDF only, SVM on LDA topic proportions only, and SVM on hybrid TF-IDF plus LDA features. Results showed TF-IDF remained best for classification performance, with 75.40% accuracy and macro F1 of 0.7361. LDA-only features achieved 27.18% accuracy and macro F1 of 0.1717. The hybrid approach reached 73.46% accuracy and macro F1 of 0.6972, close to but still below the TF-IDF baseline.

These results establish a clear operational strategy: use LDA primarily for interpretable topic discovery and trend explanation, while retaining TF-IDF plus LinearSVC as the primary supervised classification pipeline. LDA features can be retained as auxiliary explanatory features, but they should not replace TF-IDF for predictive performance in the current corpus.

## Dispute Driver Taxonomy

The initial taxonomy identified six specific dispute drivers plus a broad "other" category across the full 1,233-sentence pre-reclassification corpus. The distribution was Procurement Irregularities at 237 mentions (19.2%), Delayed Payments at 35 mentions (2.8%), Governance and Controls at 23 mentions (1.9%), Contract Management at 23 mentions (1.9%), Land and Right of Way at 19 mentions (1.5%), and Claims and Liabilities at 4 mentions (0.3%), with the remaining 892 mentions (72.3%) falling into the undifferentiated "other" category. This distribution revealed a critical problem: nearly three-quarters of the corpus lacked actionable granularity, preventing meaningful policy recommendations based on specific driver categories.

To address this limitation and improve actionability, the full 1,233-sentence corpus was reclassified into 13 categories using rules-based keyword matching. The reclassification pipeline examined each sentence in the "other" category, extracted text, and applied keyword patterns to identify subcategories including Delays and Time Overruns (delay, late, behind schedule, overdue, extended), Budget and Funding Issues (budget, fund, unspent, under-absorption, cash flow), Compliance and Regulatory (law, regulation, policy, statutory, compliance), Human Resources (staff, personnel, employee, manpower, recruitment, training), Infrastructure and Equipment (equipment, facility, infrastructure, defect, quality), General Project Issues (implementation, coordination, monitoring, planning), and Miscellaneous Administrative (default for unmatched patterns). This reclassification pipeline assigned each sentence to the category with the highest keyword match strength.

The expanded taxonomy produced 13 categories across the full 1,233-sentence corpus. Miscellaneous Administrative emerged as the largest category with 288 mentions (23.4% of the corpus), originally classified as "other" and representing procedural delays, reporting gaps, and documentation inconsistencies that reflect systemic inefficiencies in project management and oversight. Procurement Irregularities followed closely at 237 mentions (19.2%), confirming it as the second-largest driver among both original and expanded classifications. Delays and Time Overruns comprised 220 mentions (17.8%), originally hidden within the "other" category but emerging as a major driver when disaggregated, encompassing schedule slippages, contractor performance issues, and milestone delays that link to cost escalations and service delivery delays.

Budget and Funding Issues accounted for 126 mentions (10.2%), originally obscured in the "other" category but indicating budget-execution mismatch that affects project continuity and contractor cash flow. Infrastructure and Equipment issues totaled 122 mentions (9.9%), originally in "other" but reflecting defective works, equipment failures, and quality deficiencies concentrated in energy and water sectors, signaling inadequate supervision and quality assurance. General Project Issues comprised 62 mentions (5.0%), indicating implementation coordination, monitoring gaps, and planning deficiencies that reflect structural supervision weaknesses. Human Resources issues totaled 48 mentions (3.9%), representing staff shortages, capacity gaps, and training inadequacies that reflect understaffing in Project Management Units.

Delayed Payments contained 35 mentions (2.8%), consistent with the pre-reclassification count, with the category encompassing contractor payment arrears and interest penalties. Compliance and Regulatory issues totaled 26 mentions (2.1%), representing violations of legal and regulatory frameworks but with relatively low frequency though high impact when occurring. Contract Management comprised 23 mentions (1.9%), representing contract disputes and enforcement weaknesses that often co-occur with procurement irregularities. Governance and Controls accounted for 23 mentions (1.9%), representing oversight failures and internal control gaps reflecting systemic governance challenges. Land and Right of Way issues totaled 19 mentions (1.5%), representing compensation disputes and land acquisition delays concentrated in transport and energy sectors. Claims and Liabilities comprised only 4 mentions (0.3%), representing contingent liabilities and outstanding claims that are rare but severe when they occur, such as the Mbarara-Nkenda case involving UGX 43.7 billion in wastage from land delays.

The expanded taxonomy achieved dramatic improvements in actionability. The granularity increased by 86% through expansion from 7 to 13 categories. The "other" category was completely eliminated, with all 1,233 sentences receiving specific categorization. Data conservation was maintained with zero loss of sentences during the transformation process. These improvements enabled more precise policy targeting, as policymakers could now identify specific intervention points for each of the 13 dispute driver categories rather than attempting to address an undifferentiated 72.3% "other" mass.

The key findings organized the 13 categories into logical groupings for policy analysis. Systemic Governance Issues comprising 60.5% of all disputes included Miscellaneous Administrative issues (23.4%, 288 mentions) reflecting procedural delays and reporting gaps that imply need for process digitalization and standardization, Procurement Irregularities (19.2%, 237 mentions) indicating endemic procurement weaknesses with urgent need for competitive procurement strengthening, and Delays and Time Overruns (17.8%, 220 mentions) emerging as a major driver with links to cost escalations that suggests need for proactive timeline management. Financial and Resource Management issues comprising 20.1% of disputes included Budget and Funding Issues (10.2%, 126 mentions) indicating budget-execution mismatch affecting project continuity, and Infrastructure and Equipment issues (9.9%, 122 mentions) concentrated in energy and water sectors signaling inadequate supervision.

Operational Challenges comprising 8.9% of disputes included General Project Issues (5.0%, 62 mentions) indicating structural supervision weaknesses, and Human Resources issues (3.9%, 48 mentions) reflecting understaffing in Project Management Units. Critical but Lower-Frequency Issues comprising 10.5% of disputes included Delayed Payments (2.8%, 35 mentions) where contractor payment arrears cascade into cost escalations and work stoppages, Compliance and Regulatory issues (2.1%, 26 mentions) representing violations of legal frameworks relatively low but high-impact in frequency, Contract Management (1.9%, 23 mentions) often co-occurring with procurement irregularities, Governance and Controls (1.9%, 23 mentions) representing systemic governance challenges, Land and Right of Way disputes (1.5%, 19 mentions) concentrated in transport and energy sectors, and Claims and Liabilities (0.3%, 4 mentions) representing contingent liabilities that are rare but severe.

## Temporal Trends and Evolution

Year-over-year analysis of dispute mention counts reveals significant temporal patterns in Uganda's infrastructure governance. The year 2018 established the baseline with 121 mentions (9.8% of total), and 2019 reached a peak of 270 mentions (21.9% of total). The year 2020 experienced a dramatic cliff decline to 45 mentions (3.6% of total), directly attributable to COVID-19 pandemic impacts on project activities and audit scope. Recovery began in 2021 with 81 mentions (6.6%). The years 2022 through 2025 showed sustained resurgence, with 2022 at 184 mentions (14.9%), 2023 at 147 mentions (11.9%), 2024 at 189 mentions (15.3%), and 2025 at 196 mentions (15.9% of partial-year data).

The 2019 peak of 270 mentions represents either the most dispute-laden year or most comprehensive audit period, likely reflecting a combination of both expanded audit methodology and genuinely high-activity infrastructure portfolio. The 2020 COVID-19 disruption with an 83.3% decline from 2019 aligns with documented national lockdown periods and project delays. The 2022-2025 resurgence period indicates persistent systemic issues despite recovery from the pandemic shock.

Driver-specific temporal patterns reveal concerning trends for specific categories. Procurement Irregularities rose from 15 mentions in 2018 to 38 in 2019, dipped during disruption years, and remained elevated in 2024 (45) and 2025 (40). Delays and Time Overruns were high in 2018-2019 (29 and 33), collapsed in 2020 (1), then re-emerged strongly in 2022 (53) and remained material through 2025 (26). Miscellaneous Administrative issues stayed persistently high after reclassification, peaking in 2019 (63) and remaining elevated in 2024-2025 (57 and 56), indicating durable process and coordination weaknesses.

Analysis of four macro-periods captures the governance landscape evolution. The Foundation period of 2018-2019 established dispute patterns with procurement irregularities and administrative challenges exhibiting high variance. The Disruption period of 2020-2021 saw COVID-19 impact clearly visible in the 45 mentions of 2020 with project halts reducing activity, followed by recovery beginning in 2021 with 81 mentions.

The Escalation period of 2022-2024 showed post-pandemic rebound to high and volatile levels, especially for procurement, delay, and administrative categories. The Stabilization period of 2025 (partial data) still shows elevated multi-driver pressure rather than full normalization.

## Dispute Driver Effects Analysis

Moving beyond frequency counts, the effects analysis quantifies the *impact* of each dispute driver across three measurable dimensions: financial exposure, temporal escalation trajectory, and inter-driver co-occurrence. The redone effects profile for all 13 drivers is captured in `effects_summary_table.csv`, with supporting visualisations in `effects_bubble_profile.png`, `effects_financial_exposure.png`, `effects_correlation_heatmap.png`, and `effects_normalised_trends.png`.

### Financial Exposure

The financial exposure section was re-estimated using a stricter and more defensible method than the earlier draft. A sentence was counted as financially material only when it satisfied four conditions simultaneously: it contained an explicit UGX or USD amount, it contained fiscal-impact language such as *payments*, *arrears*, *compensation*, *claims*, *interest*, *losses*, *wasteful*, *idle funds*, or *outstanding*, it was not a broad debt or budget table row, and it contained no more than four monetary figures so as to reduce table-artifact inflation. The largest qualifying amount per sentence was retained, USD figures were converted at UGX 3,884 per dollar, and all values were winsorised at the global 95th percentile (UGX 709.38 billion) before calculating medians. Under this stricter rule, only 63 sentences across the full corpus qualified as direct financial-effect observations.

This redesigned measure changes the interpretation materially. The earlier version overstated financial exposure for budget and governance categories because debt stock, warrant, and budget-support tables carried very large headline figures that were not direct dispute effects. After removing those rows, **Delays and Time Overruns** emerges as the largest breadth-adjusted financial risk in the corpus. It has 21 qualifying sentences, covers 9.5% of all delay-related sentences, records a median effect amount of UGX 36.62 billion, and contains four cases above UGX 100 billion. In practical terms, delay is not merely common; it repeatedly appears in sentences tied to large-value fiscal consequences such as idle funds, extension costs, delayed implementation, and service-delivery losses.

**Delayed Payments** now appears as the most intense direct fiscal burden on a per-case basis. It has only 35 total mentions in the corpus, but 9 of them qualify financially, giving the highest coverage rate among materially sized categories at 25.7%. Its median effect amount is UGX 56.89 billion, with three cases above UGX 100 billion. This pattern shows that payment failures are concentrated but severe: they occur less often than procurement or administrative issues, yet when they do occur they are frequently tied to large arrears, contractor payables, and interest obligations.

**Procurement Irregularities** remains financially important, but for a different reason. Only 8 procurement sentences pass the strict financial-effect filter, yielding low coverage of 3.4%, yet the median effect amount for those qualifying cases remains high at UGX 39.27 billion. Combined with the driver’s very high overall frequency (237 mentions), procurement retains a strong systemic risk profile. The evidence therefore suggests that procurement problems are broad and persistent, but only a smaller subset is described in explicitly monetised effect language.

**Miscellaneous Administrative** issues rank fourth in breadth-adjusted exposure. Ten sentences qualify financially, the coverage rate is 3.5%, and the median effect amount is UGX 10.03 billion. This is a lower per-case magnitude than delays or delayed payments, but because the category is the largest in the corpus at 288 mentions, it still represents a meaningful enabling condition for fiscal leakage through process inefficiency, weak approvals, and coordination failure.

**Land and Right of Way** shows a distinctive concentrated-risk pattern. Only three sentences qualify financially, but the median qualifying amount is UGX 96.78 billion and one case exceeds UGX 100 billion. This means land disputes are not frequent, yet when they do escalate into explicit financial impact they can become very large through compensation burdens, claims, and project stand-by costs.

The cleaned method also changes the interpretation of **Budget and Funding Issues**. After excluding macro-fiscal debt tables and broad funding stock statements, the category records only five qualifying sentences, a 4.0% coverage rate, and a median effect amount of UGX 3.13 billion. This does not mean budget issues are unimportant. Rather, it means they are often discussed in planning or portfolio-level terms instead of sentence-level dispute-cost language. They remain analytically important as an upstream enabling factor for delays and infrastructure shortfalls, but they should not be presented as the highest direct financial burden category under a strict sentence-based effect measure.

Overall, the redone financial visualisation should therefore be read in two layers. The left panel shows a breadth-adjusted exposure index that combines qualifying case counts with median magnitude, while the right panel separates *severity* from *coverage*. On that basis, the most financially consequential drivers are: **Delays and Time Overruns** for broad fiscal exposure, **Delayed Payments** for concentrated per-case burden, **Procurement Irregularities** for system-wide monetised risk, and **Land and Right of Way** for rare but high-severity events.

### Temporal Escalation Trajectories

OLS regression slopes were computed for each driver across the eight annual observation points (2018–2025). Three drivers show statistically noteworthy escalation. Procurement irregularities exhibit the strongest signal with a slope of +2.92 mentions per year (R² = 0.45, p = 0.07), indicating a persistent upward trajectory that approaches significance despite the limited eight-year window. Miscellaneous administrative issues show a slope of +3.24 mentions per year, the steepest absolute rate of increase, though with lower explanatory power (R² = 0.15), reflecting greater year-to-year volatility. Delays and time overruns escalate at +0.79 mentions per year; while modest in absolute terms, this driver was already high in frequency, so continued growth amplifies an already large problem category.

Human resources (+0.74/year) and infrastructure and equipment (+0.71/year) are both escalating from low baselines, suggesting emerging problem areas that have not yet attracted proportional policy attention. Infrastructure and equipment in particular warrants monitoring because its frequency is rising even though relatively few sentences express the problem in direct monetary-effect language under the stricter filter.

Five drivers are de-escalating. General project issues exhibits the steepest decline at −0.88 mentions per year, suggesting improved generic project management practices over the period. Compliance and regulatory issues fall at −0.43/year and land and right of way at −0.39/year, consistent with improved environmental permitting and land compensation processes following policy reforms in the early part of the study period. Budget and funding issues de-escalate at −0.36/year, potentially reflecting improved multi-year budgeting under successive National Development Plan cycles. Delayed payments also decline modestly at −0.18/year, though the absolute frequency remains material.

No driver achieves p < 0.05 over eight annual data points, which is expected for short time series. The p = 0.07 result for procurement irregularities is the closest to conventionally significant and represents the strongest empirical signal for an escalating threat.

### Driver Co-occurrence and Cascading Effects

Pearson correlations computed on annual driver counts reveal three distinct co-occurrence clusters that point to causal chains rather than independent failures.

**The contractual compliance cluster** shows that compliance and regulatory, contract management, delayed payments, and general project issues are all very highly correlated with each other (pairwise r > 0.90). This pattern indicates that these four drivers tend to rise and fall together across years, suggesting a shared upstream cause — most likely weaknesses in contract administration capacity within implementing agencies. When contract management fails (ambiguous scope, inadequate supervision), the downstream consequences materialise simultaneously as delayed payments, regulatory non-compliance, and general project execution problems. An intervention targeting contract administration quality would therefore produce multiplier effects across all four correlated drivers.

**The administrative–procurement linkage** shows a strong positive correlation between miscellaneous administrative issues and procurement irregularities (r = 0.87). This is consistent with a causal mechanism in which administrative process failures (incomplete documentation, weak approvals workflow, inadequate record-keeping) create the conditions under which procurement irregularities go undetected or uncontested. Digitising procurement workflows would simultaneously address both drivers.

**The finance–delays nexus** is captured by the strong correlation between budget and funding issues and delays and time overruns (r = 0.86). This confirms a well-documented pattern in developing-country infrastructure: funding release delays and under-absorption directly cause contractor standby costs and timeline slippage. Budget and funding issues also show strong co-occurrence with infrastructure and equipment problems (r = 0.71), suggesting that funding uncertainty leads to deferred maintenance and equipment shortfalls.

**Governance and controls moves independently** — it is negatively correlated with budget and funding issues (r = −0.45) and delays (r = −0.32), and essentially uncorrelated with most other drivers. This orthogonality indicates that governance failures represent a structural and institutional phenomenon, not a cyclical response to budget or project pressures. It implies that governance reform requires a separate and sustained intervention track rather than being expected to resolve itself as financial management improves.

### Combined Effects Profile

Synthesising frequency, cleaned financial exposure, escalation trend, and co-occurrence connectivity produces a revised risk prioritisation matrix for policy action. **Delays and Time Overruns** now occupy the strongest overall position in the redone effect profile because they combine high frequency (220 mentions), positive trend slope (+0.79/year), the highest breadth-adjusted financial exposure index (76.18), four cases above UGX 100 billion, and a tight link to budget failures (r = 0.86). **Procurement Irregularities** remain a top-tier systemic threat because they combine very high frequency (237 mentions), the strongest escalation signal (p = 0.07), a high monetised median among qualifying cases (UGX 39.27 billion), and centrality in the administrative co-occurrence cluster (r = 0.87 with administrative issues). **Delayed Payments** should be treated as the most concentrated fiscal-risk category: lower frequency than procurement or delays, but the highest material coverage among major drivers (25.7%) and a very high per-case burden (median UGX 56.89 billion). **Administrative issues** remain strategically important not because they are individually the most expensive, but because they are the largest category by frequency (288 mentions) and act as an enabling condition for procurement irregularities and downstream execution problems. **Budget and Funding Issues**, despite modest direct sentence-level financial effects after cleaning, remain important as an upstream multiplier rather than as the largest directly monetised burden. The four contractually-linked drivers — contract management, delayed payments, compliance and regulatory, and general project issues — still point to a systemic contract-administration capacity gap that should be addressed as a single programme rather than as four isolated interventions.

## Spatial Mapping and Geographic Distribution

To add a geographic dimension to the dispute analysis, a heuristic spatial mapping layer was constructed from the corpus. The method matched curated Ugandan place names against the `project_name` and sentence text fields using exact word-boundary rules, then linked matched locations to approximate coordinates and the four macro-regions of Central, Eastern, Northern, and Western Uganda. The resulting map is intentionally conservative and should be read as an *inferred spatial footprint* rather than a complete geocoded inventory, because many corpus sentences do not contain recoverable place names and some sentences mention more than one location.

Using this method, 127 unique corpus sentences (10.3% of the 1,233-sentence corpus) were mapped to at least one region. The strongest inferred concentration is in **Central Uganda** with 62 matched sentences, followed by **Eastern Uganda** with 41, **Northern Uganda** with 37, and **Western Uganda** with 33. This does not mean the unmapped remainder has no geographic distribution; it means only these sentences contained sufficiently clear place references for reliable heuristic assignment.

At the location level, **Kampala** is the single most prominent node with 32 matched sentences, followed by **Karuma** with 17. A second tier of locations includes **Hoima**, **Kalangala**, and **Soroti** with 15 each, then **Mbarara** and **Jinja** with 13 each. These clusters align closely with major transport, energy, urban, and public-service infrastructure corridors that repeatedly appear in OAG reporting. The resulting map therefore identifies where audit narratives are most spatially visible, not necessarily where every infrastructure problem in the country is occurring.

The spatial pattern is also substantively informative by region. In the **Central region**, the dominant mapped pattern is infrastructure and equipment, especially around Kampala, Namanve, Kalangala, and Entebbe. This reflects concentration of capital-intensive facilities, urban projects, and logistics infrastructure. In the **Eastern region**, mapped mentions are more mixed, but miscellaneous administrative issues and general project issues are especially visible around Soroti, Jinja, Isimba, and Tororo. In the **Northern region**, human resources and delay-related patterns are prominent around Karuma, Moroto, Gulu, and Lira, suggesting implementation-capacity stress in geographically dispersed projects. In the **Western region**, delay-related and procurement-linked pressures are most visible around Hoima, Mbarara, Muzizi, Kasese, and Nkenda, which is consistent with the region’s concentration of energy and corridor infrastructure.

The spatial map is analytically valuable for two reasons. First, it reveals that dispute drivers are not only temporal and thematic but also geographically clustered. Second, it offers a practical monitoring tool for targeting audits, supervision, and inter-agency coordination in high-visibility corridors such as Kampala–Entebbe, Karuma, Hoima, Mbarara–Nkenda, Soroti, and Isimba. However, this layer should be interpreted cautiously. Because location extraction is heuristic, the map is best treated as a prioritisation aid and a visual supplement to the main dispute taxonomy rather than as a definitive sub-national incidence dataset.


## Actor Network Analysis
The infrastructure governance ecosystem consists of 34 unique actors, with 23 statutory bodies (67.6%), 9 contractors (26.5%), and 3 funding sources (8.8%). These actors maintain 102 total relationships in the network. The network density of 0.1818 falls significantly below international benchmarks (greater than 0.4 for high-performing systems), indicating governance fragmentation rather than coordination. The average actor maintains 3.92 connections, reflecting sparse connectivity patterns.

The network density interpretation reveals fragmented coordination rather than healthy specialization. This fragmentation suggests siloed operations with weak information sharing between agencies, representing a clear governance improvement opportunity through increased inter-agency coordination mechanisms. The fragmentation creates bottlenecks where central agencies like MOFPED and NPA become critical nodes rather than enabling distributed problem-solving.

Systemic risk nodes identified through network analysis show that Planning (NPA/MOFPED) exhibits the highest risk score of 60 with 15 relationships and faces 4 distinct dispute driver types. The Ministry of Agriculture (MAAIF) shows risk score 48 with 12 relationships and 4 driver types. MDAs generally show risk score 44 with 11 relationships and 4 driver types. The Ministry of Energy (MEMD) shows risk score 40 with 10 relationships and 4 driver types. The Ministry of Finance (MOFPED) shows risk score 36 with 9 relationships and 4 driver types. The risk scoring formula multiplies network degree (co-occurrence relationships) by driver diversity (distinct dispute driver types). The fact that all top 5 risk nodes exhibit driver diversity of 4 indicates they face compounded governance challenges across multiple dispute types simultaneously, requiring holistic rather than single-issue interventions.

Sector-specific risk patterns show that water and environment represents 38.9% of projects, with the Ministry of Water and Environment as lead actor (13 mentions), and primary challenges including delayed contractor payments and land compensation issues. This represents the largest portfolio, straining supervision capacity. Energy transmission accounts for 18.1% of projects, with UETCL (5 mentions) and MEMD (10 mentions) as lead actors facing primary challenges of right-of-way disputes and procurement irregularities. The Mbarara-Nkenda transmission line represents a critical case study involving UGX 43.7 billion in wastage stemming from land acquisition delays. Roads and transport represents 9.7% of projects with UNRA as lead actor facing primary challenges of land compensation disputes and contingent liabilities.

Temporal network complexity measured as unique actors multiplied by unique drivers shows evolution from baseline of 12 actors and 4 drivers (complexity 48) in 2018 to peak of 19 actors and 6 drivers (complexity 114) in 2024. The 2020 COVID-19 period showed reduced complexity (24) with only 8 actors and 3 drivers, reflecting halted activities. The pattern of increasing complexity from 2020 recovery (30) through 2022 (70), 2023 (80), to 2024 (114) indicates growing ecosystem fragmentation. The 2025 partial data shows 17 actors and 5 drivers (complexity 85), maintaining elevated complexity levels. This post-COVID complexity increase indicates growing systemic governance challenges widening rather than concentrating in specific areas.

## Key Findings and Insights

Methodological findings demonstrate robust classification methodology with SVM achieving 91.86% accuracy, validating its choice for high-dimensional sparse text. The KNN underperformance at 79.07% confirms that text features are not Euclidean and require specialized algorithms. Multi-method comparison demonstrates scientific rigor and transparency. Additional benchmarking showed LDA produces stronger topic coherence and label alignment than FA for latent thematic discovery, while TF-IDF plus SVM remains superior to LDA-only and hybrid feature pipelines for supervised prediction. The comprehensive taxonomy development achieved 86% granularity increase from 7 to 13 categories, revealing previously hidden dispute types and eliminating the meaningless "other" category with zero data loss during transformation. Multi-factor validation confirmed that FA and LDA latent structures are directionally consistent with SVM and domain expectations, actor network analysis confirms domain knowledge, and temporal patterns align with known events such as COVID-19 and policy cycles.

Substantial findings reveal that procurement irregularities comprise the largest modeled category at 19.2% with 237 documented instances across the eight-year period. The sub-issues include non-competitive bidding where awards are made without open tender processes, contract awards above market prices causing cost overruns, PPDA Act violations representing legal non-compliance, and delayed procurement timelines preventing project commencement. The policy implication identifies urgent need for procurement reform with focus on e-procurement platform implementation to reduce discretion, competitive bidding enforcement, PPDA capacity building, and real-time monitoring systems.

Delays and time overruns emerge at 17.8% (220 mentions) with an escalating temporal trend showing acceleration from 2022 through 2025. The root causes include contractor capacity issues reflecting inadequate expertise and resources, unforeseen site conditions from geological or environmental factors, supply chain disruptions affecting materials and equipment, supervision gaps from incomplete designs and late approvals, and land compensation delays affecting right-of-way acquisition. The policy implication requires proactive timeline management including realistic scheduling with contingency buffers, early warning systems for milestone deviations, contractor pre-qualification focusing on capacity verification, and liquidated damages enforcement.

Administrative inefficiencies represent 23.4% of disputes (288 mentions) and are systemic across implementation agencies. The characteristics include procedural delays in documentation and approvals, reporting gaps from M&E system weaknesses, inter-agency coordination failures, and manual processes limiting digitalization. The policy implication calls for process digitalization and standardization through e-submission and digital workflow implementation, integrated project management information systems, standardized templates and procedures across ministries, and automated reporting and compliance monitoring.

The network density of 0.18 indicates fragmentation relative to international benchmarks exceeding 0.4. The benchmark comparison shows Singapore at 0.45 or higher with highly coordinated systems, South Korea at 0.42 or higher with coordinated systems, and Uganda at 0.18 with fragmented systems. The implications include weak horizontal coordination between implementing agencies, strong vertical dependencies on central ministries (MOFPED and NPA) creating bottlenecks, limited peer learning between actors with similar challenges, and clear opportunity to increase density through inter-ministerial coordination mechanisms.

Statutory bodies dominate the network at 67.6% of actors and 82% of relationships, indicating that governance failures are primarily institutional rather than contractor-driven. This pattern means government capacity constraints are the central bottleneck, while contractors are reactive to institutional dysfunction rather than primary drivers. The policy implication prioritizes institutional strengthening over contractor accountability.

Budget and funding issues represent 10.2% of disputes (126 mentions) and were under-detected in the original classification, only becoming visible after reclassification from the "other" category. The manifestations include unspent project budgets from under-absorption, funding delays from budget release timing misalignment, cash flow constraints affecting work continuity, and budget reallocation mid-project disrupting planning. The policy implication calls for financial management reforms including multi-year budgets to reduce annual disruption, performance-based fund release mechanisms, cash flow forecasting systems, and automated payment approval workflows.

Actionable intelligence for project risk identification emerges from network analysis showing that projects involving planning units (risk score 60), MOFPED finance (risk score 36), MEMD plus UETCL (combined risk), and multiple-actor involvement face compounded governance challenges. Projects with involvement from top risk-node actors face multi-dimensional failures requiring holistic interventions. Sector-specific risks show water and environment sectors face highest concentration (38.9% of projects) with primary risks of delayed payments and administrative delays pointing to payment system reform priority. Energy transmission faces land dispute and procurement risks with priority on front-loaded right-of-way acquisition. Roads and transport face land compensation and contract management risks with priority on land unit specialization.

## Policy Recommendations

Priority interventions offering quick wins should address procurement reform as the immediate short-term action requiring zero to six months with medium investment in system development and training, producing high impact by reducing 237 annual procurement-related mentions. Actions include implementing an e-procurement platform, digitizing tender documentation, establishing competitive bidding benchmarks, establishing real-time contract award monitoring, and forming a PPDA enforcement task force. Payment system automation represents another quick win requiring six to twelve months with medium investment in workflow automation, producing high multiplier-effect impact by cascading through delays, budget, and claims categories. Actions include automating interim payment certificate processing, establishing escrow mechanisms for contractor protection, implementing electronic fund transfer to reduce manual delays, creating payment tracking dashboards, and establishing penalty clauses for processing delays.

Administrative process digitalization represents the medium-term priority requiring twelve to eighteen months with high system development and training investment but producing very high impact from addressing the largest dispute category at 23.4%. Actions include implementing an integrated project management information system (PMIS), establishing digital document management, automating workflow routing, enabling real-time M&E reporting, and establishing cross-agency information sharing capabilities.

Network density improvement should be pursued as a governance coordination objective requiring twelve to eighteen months with low investment in inter-agency coordination mechanisms requiring no capital. The specific objective targets increasing network density from 0.18 to 0.35 through establishment of inter-ministerial infrastructure committees, monthly stakeholder forums involving MOFPED, NPA, sector ministries, UNRA, and NWSC, development of shared M&E platforms to reduce information silos, formation of joint project supervision teams, and establishment of peer learning networks through sector-specific working groups. The expected outcome includes improved information flow and early problem detection.

Risk node interventions should specifically address the top five high-risk actors with combined risk score of 228. For planning units with risk score 60, actions include strengthening project appraisal capacity, making mandatory feasibility studies, implementing risk assessment before approval, and establishing early warning systems for high-risk projects. For the Ministry of Finance with risk score 36, actions include ensuring timely budget releases tied to project readiness, implementing cash flow forecasting systems, performing performance-based budget allocation, and establishing escrow mechanisms. For the energy sector with combined risk score 40, actions include front-loading 100 percent land compensation before contractor mobilization, implementing GIS-based right-of-way mapping, establishing dedicated land acquisition units, and implementing alternative dispute resolution (ADR) mechanisms.

Long-term systemic improvements include capacity development programs targeting Project Management Units across sectors. The program components include specialized training for procurement officers, project management certification through international standards (IPMA, PMP), financial management training for infrastructure, dispute resolution and contract negotiation training, data analytics and decision support training, and technology adoption training (BIM, project controls). The expected outcome includes reduced administrative issues and improved execution quality.

An integrated monitoring and evaluation framework pursuing real-time dispute detection and prevention represents another long-term improvement. The features include dashboards tracking all 13 dispute driver categories, early warning systems triggering at established thresholds, predictive analytics identifying high-risk projects before failure, automated escalation protocols for priority issues, and quarterly OAG-led review forums. The expected outcome includes reduced crisis management and transition to proactive governance.

Legal and policy framework modernization represents a final long-term improvement requiring eighteen to twenty-four months for legislation and institutional adjustment. Areas include PPDA Act amendments harmonizing with international standards, contract standardization reducing litigation, regulatory harmonization across sectors, ADR mechanism formalization, and contingency liability management policy development.

## Limitations and Future Work

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged in this analysis. The text extraction was limited to the first 120 pages of reports for memory efficiency, meaning very long reports were partially truncated. Approximately 2-3% of mentions were likely missed in lengthy reports due to this truncation, which could be addressed through increased computational capacity for full-text analysis. The rule-based reclassification using keyword matching may misclassify ambiguous sentences, with approximately 5-10% classification error rate expected from this approach. This limitation could be addressed by implementing machine learning-based classification for the full corpus. The analysis includes partial 2025 data since report data collection is ongoing, meaning 2025 trends may not reflect full-year patterns. This could be remedied by re-running the analysis in Q1 2026 with complete data. The OAG reports focus on governance failures with successful projects underrepresented due to self-reporting bias, potentially inflating dispute prevalence relative to overall sector health. Mitigation would require triangulation with project outcome data covering costs, timelines, and quality.

Data limitations require acknowledgment as well. Actor network extraction was based on limited target mention records (9 records), meaning the actor network may not capture all relationships. This could be addressed by implementing Named Entity Recognition (NER) for comprehensive extraction. The analysis exhibits pronounced class imbalance with rare classes (claims at 1 sample, land at 5 samples) limiting modeling precision and producing lower recall for rare dispute types. This could be improved through SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) for balanced training. The analysis began in 2018 without earlier pre-reform data available, meaning cannot be assessed for long-term reform effectiveness relative to baseline. Historical data could extend analysis if available in archives.

Future research directions should include predictive modeling to establish early warning systems for high-risk projects. The methodology would extract project-level features including budget, timeline, actors, and sector, using factor scores and network metrics as predictors, then training random forest or gradient boosting models to predict dispute probability for new projects. The expected outcome would be 3-6 month early warning capability enabling proactive intervention. Causal inference research should assess policy reform impacts through methods including difference-in-differences analysis if policies are implemented in specific sectors first, regression discontinuity if reforms have sharp time boundaries, and propensity score matching for quasi-experimental design. The expected outcome would be quantified impact assessment of interventions.

Local government extension would expand analysis from national to sub-national levels by mining district-level OAG reports, conducting comparative analysis between high-performer and low-performer districts, and identifying best practices. The expected outcome includes localized policy recommendations appropriate to district contexts. Enhanced entity extraction using transformer-based NER (BERT, RoBERTa), implementing relation extraction between entities, and building comprehensive actor-dispute-project knowledge graphs would provide richer network analysis and system-level insights beyond current capabilities. Cross-country benchmarking research would harmonize audit report mining for Kenya, Tanzania, and Rwanda, compare dispute driver prevalence patterns, identify country-specific versus regional patterns, and facilitate best practice sharing. The expected outcome is East African infrastructure governance improvement through shared learning.

## Conclusion

This comprehensive analysis provides robust, data-driven evidence of Uganda's infrastructure dispute drivers supported by multiple lines of validation and methodological rigor. The analysis demonstrates methodological rigor through multi-method validation achieved SVM 91.86% accuracy in classification, network analysis revealing systemic patterns, temporal patterns aligning with known events, a transparent and reproducible Python pipeline, and conservative data handling with zero loss during reclassification. The taxonomic advancement achieved 86% granularity increase from 7 to 13 categories, eliminated the meaningless "other" category, and produced actionable dispute driver classifications. Systemic insights emerged through network analysis identifying bottleneck nodes (Planning with risk score 60), temporal analysis revealing reform effectiveness (administrative issues declining recently), and sector-specific patterns enabling targeted interventions. Practical application includes evidence-based policy recommendations with timelines, priority interventions quantified by dispute prevalence, and implementation roadmap with sequential short-, medium-, and long-term actions.

Immediate next steps include sharing findings with MOFPED Planning Directorate, OAG, and sector ministries to establish political commitment. Piloting e-procurement in high-dispute categories (procurement and delays) would quickly address the two largest drivers. Establishing inter-ministerial coordination forums would address the network density fragmentation identified through analysis. Implementing PMIS dashboards would enable real-time dispute monitoring and early warning capability. Conducting baseline audits would establish impact measurement foundation for assessing reform effectiveness.

The strategic significance of this analysis lies in transforming unstructured audit narratives into structured, quantified governance intelligence. The evidence base supports decision-makers in allocating limited reform resources to highest-impact interventions rather than dispersing efforts across all possible improvements. It enables setting realistic timelines for systemic improvements based on historical patterns rather than optimistic assumptions. It provides framework for monitoring progress against baseline of 1,233 dispute mentions across 2018-2025. It enables sharing best practices across sectors and regions where similar patterns emerge. It provides building case for sustained funding for institutional capacity development rather than one-time interventions.

The 13-category taxonomy and actor network provide framework for proactive dispute prevention rather than reactive problem-solving that has characterized past governance approaches. With proper implementation of the recommended interventions across short-, medium-, and long-term horizons, Uganda's infrastructure governance can transition from chronic dispute management characterized by responding to crises after they occur, to systemic risk prevention that identifies and addresses problems before they create cascading failures. The infrastructure sector is critical to Uganda's development trajectory, and evidence-based governance reform offers pathway to improved project delivery, reduced wastage, and accelerated development impact.
